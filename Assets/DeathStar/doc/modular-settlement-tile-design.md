# Modular Settlement Exterior Tile Design

Design proposal — 27 October 2026

Build a modular space station from visually distinct cubes. Each cube has a recognizable purpose, and the player should be able to identify its type from its exterior while building. A Habitat cube is the home of one person. The station can grow into an outpost or a larger structure; its look draws on the Death Star and Star Destroyers without having to be either.

**The defining visual rule is that every tile family has a clear exterior signature. Habitat cubes carry two rows of windows on each face across their axis. Other families get equally strong identifying features.** Function guides the exterior, but this design focuses on the visible shell, openings, equipment, silhouette, and relationships between neighboring cubes.

## A station in space

The settlement is a station in vacuum, not a group of buildings on ground. There is no floor plane, no weather, and no gravity outside. Leave out exterior features that only make sense on ground: canopies, steps down to a street, roof parapets and railings, and front doors onto open ground. People move between tiles through corridors and airlocks. Ships arrive at landing pads, docking collars, and hangars.

The station's own armour is its ground. Tiles merge into one monolithic armoured mass rather than standing as separate buildings. Where cubes are missing, the gaps become trenches; where cubes stack, they become towers and stepped tiers.

The look is brutalist and heavy:

- thick walls with deep recesses;
- few, small openings apart from the window rows;
- strong repetition;
- quiet, heavy family forms set against a busy relief;
- hard sunlight with deep shadows.

Grit and wear come in a later detail pass.

## Scale and human reference

Use a nominal cube approximately **5 metres on each side**. This is a proposed working scale for the human-sized settlement.

| Reference | Dimension or relationship |
| --- | --- |
| Cube exterior | Approximately 5 m × 5 m × 5 m |
| Gross footprint | 25 m² |
| Reference person | 1.8 m tall, or 36% of cube height |
| Human doorway | Approximately 1 m wide × 2.1 m high |
| Visible corridor opening | Approximately 1.5–2 m wide × 2.4 m high |
| Floor | One room per cube, its floor the face towards the station's core; about 4.2 m clear to the ceiling |
| Sliding door | 1.3 m wide × 2.2 m high, at floor level between rooms side by side |
| Lift | A round opening 1.8 m across in floor and ceiling between rooms stacked out from the core |
| Window row | Two per face across the tile's axis, on a 2.5 m pitch along it: sill about 0.9 m and head about 1.85 m above the start of each band, panes on a 1.25 m pitch |
| Habitat window row | Taller panes: sill about 0.6 m and head about 2.1 m above the start of each band |
| Airlock hatch | Round, approximately 1.2–1.4 m across |
| Landing pad | About 4.4 m across on one cube; about 9 m across on a 2 × 2 block |
| Pod (scale craft) | About 6 m long and 2.6 m wide; docks on a single pad |
| Shuttle (scale craft) | About 11 m long with a 10 m span; docks on a 2 × 2 pad or at a hangar mouth |
| Structure 20 cubes across | Approximately 100 m at nominal spacing |

Doors, window rows, handrails where needed, access hatches, and equipment panels establish the human scale. A single door should look appropriately sized for a person beside the cube. Repeated windows are expected: they run in 2.5 m bands along each tile's axis, so they read as rows of lit rooms rather than as an arbitrary grid.

At a view where a cube is 100 pixels high, a 1.8 m reference person would be roughly 36 pixels high at the same depth. This is a scale where homes and individual installations can be understood directly.

Craft establish scale for ships the way a person does for rooms. The review stage docks two small craft, which are props rather than tiles:

- **Pod:** a two-seat craft about 6 m long, docked on single pads.
- **Shuttle:** about 11 m long, docked on the 2 × 2 pad or at a hangar mouth.

Each has a hatch ring under its belly that sits over the pad's airlock. They check that a single pad suits a small pod, a 2 × 2 pad a shuttle, and a hangar mouth admits one.

On the curved Foundry grid a cell's faces measure 2.3–5 m and its depth 2.8–5.6 m, depending on direction. Parts people and craft use keep their size in metres everywhere: doors, hatches, window rows, lifts and stairs, corridor and conduit cross-sections, collars, portholes, railings and pads. Turrets, dishes, tanks, fin banks, globes and masts come in small, medium and large sizes; a cell gets the largest that fits. Walls, piers, edge bands, plinths, relief, armour carving, fillets and buttresses stretch to fill the rest. Repeated parts change in number, not in size.

The earlier kilometre-sized district concept is superseded. Districts now emerge from groups of small cubes.

## No up

Outside there is no up. Every exposed face of a tile follows the same rules; no face is a roof, a floor, or a front because of where it points. A tile can be placed in any of the 24 orientations of a cube, set by two of its own axes: its axis and its side. Its forms follow those axes and the face they sit on, never a world direction.

- The tile's axis orients forms that need a direction, such as window rows, fin banks, and vaults. It is not the interior's up: inside, up is out from the station's core (see Interiors and windows).
- A family's main form (a turret, a tower, a bay, a pad) goes on its main face: the face along its axis if that face is exposed, otherwise the first exposed face around it.
- Forms without a direction, such as the relief and Power's fin banks, go on every exposed face.
- Turrets, towers, and pads can point any way, including away from the rest of the structure.

Review the station with a camera that tumbles freely, and check every family in several orientations.

## Interiors and windows

People walk inside these tiles, and every occupied tile should let them see out. Windows are therefore planned at the exterior level, as part of each family's shape, rather than added as decoration.

Inside, up is out from the station's core, whatever way a tile is turned: there is gravity indoors, and people walk upright in it. A cube holds one open room, about 4.2 m clear, whose floor is the face towards the core and whose ceiling carries its lights. On the curved grid the floors lie on the sphere's shells, so they are level; the walls follow the lattice and lean by up to about 26° near the cube's diagonals. The cells round the core itself form a zero-g hub: no floor, round hatches to every neighbour, and people float.

Neighbouring rooms whose floors face the same way open into each other: side by side through a sliding door at floor level, and stacked out from the core through a lift, a round opening in floor and ceiling where suction carries people up or down and sets them down at a floor. Rooms whose floors face different ways stay closed. A corridor's tube carries a walkway where it runs along a floor, reached from a home's floor by a stair at its socket, and is a lift shaft where it runs out from the core.

A tile's axis orients its window rows, not its floor. Each face across the axis carries two rows on a 2.5 m pitch along it, with the sill about 0.9 m and the head about 1.85 m above the start of each 2.5 m band. Habitat uses taller panes, from about 0.6 m to 2.1 m, because homes look out more than workspaces do. Every family uses the same bands, so rows on neighbouring tiles of one family that share an axis meet. The rows are real openings inside, wherever they fall: a home whose axis points out from the core has two bands of windows in its walls, and one turned across the core has upright rows in two walls and, where those faces are open, a skylight and a floor window.

| Rule | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Depth | Set the glass about 0.4 m into the armour so the wall reads as thick. |
| Pitch | Use deep mullions on a 1.25 m pitch measured in station space. Neighbouring rows line up, and a mullion falls on every tile boundary, so each pane belongs to one tile and one room. |
| Joining | A row on a face joins the same row on the neighbour of the same family. Where it does not continue, it stops short of the edge and leaves a pier. |
| Shadow line | Give each row a shallow angled visor above and a ledge below. Keep the visor shallow enough that the glass stays visible from elevated construction views. |
| Glass | Each pane shows a lit room behind it. Most rooms are lit, some dim, a few dark. |

Windows belong where people are:

| Family | Windows |
| --- | --- |
| Habitat | Both rows, with taller panes, on every exposed face across its axis |
| Command | A sloped bridge window on its tower and an upper row on its other faces |
| Corridor | Round portholes along both sides |
| Landing Pad | An upper row on its sides for the pad's control room |
| Industrial | A window band near the top of its control tower |
| Hull | A pair of small windows on some faces |
| Power, Life Support, Storage, Defense | Few or none: machinery and cargo spaces |

Round airlock hatches give people a way through the armour: a raised collar around a slightly proud door. They appear at the centre of every landing pad, at docking collars where a corridor ends in space, and on some hull faces for maintenance work outside.

## Station-wide relief

One relief pattern covers the whole station. It is real geometry, not a texture. Its plates stand 0.1–0.8 m off the face, with channels down to the face between them, and take these forms:

- raised and sunken plates, steps, and frames;
- the carved armour's plate shapes at relief scale: plates with a corner cut away at 45°, and plates split along a diagonal or a jogging groove about 10 cm wide into two parts one height step apart;
- ribs, block grids, glyph-like bars, and stepped pyramids;
- ramps, drums, domes, and rings.

Sharing the corner cuts and splits with the armour makes the relief and the family forms read as one material.

The pattern is defined in station space on every axis-aligned plane, not per tile, and it is larger than a tile. It must not read as a grid from far away, so its top level avoids long straight lines:

| Level | Layout |
| --- | --- |
| Districts | 32 m squares with a 12 m square beside each, tiled so that each row steps 12 m against the next. No large-scale line runs on for more than 44 m in either direction. The tiling is offset on every plane so that its lines never fall on the 5 m grid. Some districts are quiet fields of large plates; others are dense machinery. The 12 m square shares its district's character. |
| Plates | Each district is cut at a third to two-thirds of its width, and its long pieces are often cut again across, into two to four plates of different sizes. The 12 m square stays whole. |
| Rims | About half of these plates carry a raised rim, 0.55 m wide, on one to three of their edges but never all four, so the lines stop and start instead of closing into boxes. |

Below this level, plates split again and again into the forms listed above.

Curved pieces break the grid of plates and are set into the relief rather than laid on top of it, like the curved walkways and ring wells on the Death Star trench panels:

| Piece | Form |
| --- | --- |
| Arc | A band 1.3–2.2 m wide and 0.45–0.6 m high, curving around a radius of 4–7 m for up to about two-thirds of a circle. Cross ribs sit every 1.6 m, with blocks and small drums along its top. Angled buttresses step down its outer side and, now and then, its inner side, and a heavy block closes each free end. |
| Well | A ring wall about 5 m across and 0.6 m high, with eight angled buttresses around it and a drum at its centre. Half of the arcs run into a well; some wells stand on their own. |
| Bed | Plates under a curve, its buttresses, or the inside of a well flatten to the lowest level, 0.1 m. The curve sits in a clean channel framed by busier relief. |

Each top-level plate decides its own curves. Every face draws the curves of its own plates and their neighbours, and builds the short pieces centred on it. A curve therefore continues across tile boundaries like the rest of the relief, and every face agrees on where the bed lies.

Each tile cuts its exposed faces out of the same pattern, so the relief continues across tile boundaries with no seam. A piece that crosses a tile boundary, cut shapes included, is split there, and each tile builds its own part. Faces against solid neighbours are not built at all. The relief closes only at convex edges and where a family's form takes over part of a face.

**There are no cube seams.** The cube grid is not visible on the exterior, and tiles are told apart by their family forms rather than by their outlines. The relief follows the same rules on every plane, so it has no preferred direction either.

Family forms are quiet, heavy masses set against the busy relief, so they stand out at construction distance.

## Armour plate carving

The flat armour of the family forms is carved into real plates, like the turret towers of the Death Star trench models. It covers armour courses, fillets, lips, plinths, tiers, fins, and corridor tubes, but not the relief or the walls under it. Faces narrower than about 0.55 m stay plain.

The plates are geometry, not a texture: the carving replaces each flat armour face with plates on a floor.

Round forms stay smooth: tanks, vaults, domes, the sphere tank, pads, pipes, and the dish. The contrast between patterned armour and smooth vessels is part of the look.

### Courses

Plates are laid in courses (rows), staggered so joints do not line up. The courses stack along the tile's axis, so they wrap round a tower and every side of it matches; on the faces at the ends of the axis they stack along the tile's side. The layout lives in station space, on the plane a surface faces most, so it runs on across tiles and across forms on the same plane without a seam. Neighbouring tiles with different axes lay their courses in different directions. On faces close to 45°, neighbouring pieces can switch plane.

### Calm and busy zones

Station space is split into 8 m zones in all three directions, so that both faces at a box's corner usually fall in the same zone. About 40% of the zones are calm; the rest are busy. The mix gives quiet areas between dense detail, like the big plain plates beside tight clusters on the trench model towers.

| Zone | Courses | Plates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Busy | About 0.62 m tall | One to four cells of about 0.31 m (0.31–1.24 m long). Splits are common. |
| Calm | About 1.24 m tall | Mostly long, up to four cells of about 0.62 m (0.62–2.48 m long). Almost all are whole; few are split. |

Where a zone boundary crosses a face, a seam runs along it.

### Plate shapes

- **Plates:** each plate is raised off the face with a bevelled top, by 4, 7, or 10 cm. Seams about 3 cm wide run down to the face between them, and plates differ slightly in grey.
- **Cut corners:** some plates have one corner cut away at 45°.
- **Split plates:** a split runs along the plate's long side as a deep cut about 6 cm wide. The cut either crosses the plate diagonally or runs along it, jogging diagonally between two levels. The two parts stand 5 and 12 cm proud of the plate's height, so they read as two separate pieces.
- **Sunk panels:** a raised frame, 5–12 cm wide and 3 cm above the plate's height, round a plate set only about 1 cm off the face. A panel fits whatever part of its plate the face's edges leave.
- **Hatches:** these appear only on whole plates of at least about 0.5 × 0.8 m, never on one cut short by an edge. The door sits in a frame, with two hinge blocks on one side and a handle on the other. On a plate that is about square, the frame and door are octagonal, with a hub and a bar across the door.

The relief uses the same corner cuts and splits at a larger scale.

### Edges

A plain band about 8 cm wide runs along each face's edges, so box corners read as solid armour with the plates set in from them. Pieces that the edges would cut to slivers under about 6 cm wide are left out. The band is left off along tile boundaries, where a surface can carry on into the neighbouring tile, so it adds no seam there. As a result, a real corner that lies exactly on a tile boundary gets no band.

### Cost

The carving holds most of the stage's triangles, so it is built only where it can be seen:

- Faces that lie on a tile's own wall and face into it, such as the backs of forms set against the wall, are not built at all.
- Carving is laid out after the rest of the tile, and plates under another solid form are left out: the top of a plinth under a tower, or the foot of a box standing on another.
- From about 110 m, when a 5 m cube is under about 45 pixels tall at 1080p, a tile swaps to a copy with its armour plain. The carving there would be finer than a pixel and read as noise.

## Recognition before decoration

Give each family three identifying features:

1. **A dominant feature:** a window row, shutter, machine assembly, radiator bank, tank group, antenna, turret, landing pad, pipe bundle, or passage opening.
2. **A characteristic arrangement:** bands across the tile's axis, repeated units along it, an asymmetrical machine cluster, a central recess, or a stepped profile on the main face.
3. **A supporting material pattern:** glazing, ribs, armour, service panels, or quiet plate surfaces.

The dominant feature must remain obvious without hovering, reading a label, or enabling a color overlay. Small pipes, screws, decals, and lights support the identity; they cannot carry it by themselves.

Each type should remain distinguishable in grayscale. Color adds character and helps selection, while silhouette and large surface features do the identification work.

## Exterior treatment by type

### Habitat

Rows of windows are the defining feature: two rows on every exposed face across the tile's axis, one in each 2.5 m band along it, following the rules under Interiors and windows. Habitat panes are taller than the standard row, about 1.5 m from sill to head, so homes read as lit rooms even from the overview. Deep mullions, an angled visor over each row, and a ledge under it make the rows read as lit rooms from the normal building view, even where the panes themselves are small at that distance.

Give glass a clear material contrast with the surrounding shell: dark or subtly tinted panes, a readable reflection response, and soft illumination from the room behind. Keep mullions and visors thick enough to survive at a distance. The exterior can communicate habitation without furnishing or exposing the space behind the glass.

Keep the rest of the shell quiet, with limited exterior equipment. Habitat should be among the quieter tiles. There is no front door onto open ground: people enter from a neighbouring Corridor, from neighbouring homes through sliding doors and lifts, or through an airlock hatch. Neighbouring homes that share an axis join their rows into continuous bands; stacked homes add a row every 2.5 m, so a block of homes reads as a wall of lit windows.

Variants can change the visor shape, the mullion depth, or the ledge while retaining both window rows. A Habitat variant should not lose its windows and become indistinguishable from Hull.

### Industrial

The signature is machinery attached to or recessed into the cube. Use a small number of convincing assemblies: a ribbed housing connected to pipes, a cylinder held by brackets, or an equipment recess crossed by a gantry.

Favor asymmetry and a varied depth profile. One face may have a substantial service door while another carries exposed equipment. Industrial should be the busiest family, with overlapping forms and strong local shadows, but its machinery must still form readable groups.

Use limited wear around access points and equipment connections. Avoid filling every surface with unrelated small blocks. At this scale, every large protrusion should resemble a machine a person could service.

### Power and Thermal

Use regular banks of heavy equipment as the signature: repeated finned housings, recessed cooling ribs, or modules joined by thick conduits. The repetition distinguishes Power from Industrial's more irregular machinery.

Give the assemblies a strong vertical or horizontal rhythm and a clear relationship between input, equipment, and output. Small status lights may reinforce the identity, but the tile should remain recognizable with all lights off.

A 2 × 2 block of Power tiles becomes a reactor dome between radiator wings.

Power and cooling can begin as variants of one family. Cooling variants emphasize fins and heat-exchange surfaces; storage and distribution variants emphasize enclosed repeated modules and conduits. Exterior grilles communicate equipment housing, without implying that ordinary air ventilation provides cooling in vacuum.

### Life Support

Use grouped cylindrical tanks, rounded filter housings, and broad ducts as the signature. Secure them visibly to a regular supporting frame. Keep the pipe network organized so this family feels like controlled processing equipment.

The form depends on how many Life Support tiles stand together:

| Arrangement | Form |
| --- | --- |
| A single tile | Two banded upright tanks against each face across its axis, and four domed tank tops on its axial faces. It reads as a cluster of vessels rather than one rounded lump. |
| A row | Half-cylinder vaults with rings run along the row and close with domed ends where it stops. |
| A 2 × 2 block | A single sphere tank (see 2 × 2 landmarks). |

The rounded forms distinguish Life Support from the rectangular Power banks and the mixed machinery of Industrial. Variants can emphasize water tanks, air-processing ducts, or recycling equipment while keeping the grouped-vessel silhouette.

### Storage and Logistics

A large cargo shutter or loading door dominates the main face. Use a deep rectangular frame, broad threshold, and simple reinforcing ribs. Other faces remain relatively quiet, with container-like panels and a few identification markings.

The shutter should read as a major opening rather than a small personal entrance. Use broad slats or ribs so its pattern remains visible from the normal camera distance.

Adjacent Logistics cubes can share loading frames, reinforcing bands, or a continuous handling frontage. A hangar develops across several cubes rather than appearing as a miniature ship hangar on every tile. A row merges its bays into one slot, and a 2 × 2 block opens one hangar mouth large enough for a shuttle.

### Defense and Security

Use an armoured base and a distinct protective or outward-facing feature. Weapon variants can carry a small turret or recessed gun mount; security variants can use a fortified observation slit and strongly reinforced entrance.

Thick plates, angular protective cheeks, and separated equipment establish the silhouette. Keep the surrounding surface simpler so the emplacement stands out. Where a weapon is present, its facing direction should be visually clear.

The tile is a short tower, like the turret towers of the Death Star trench models. On each face across its axis, three sloped armour courses step in towards the main face. The main face carries the turret on a stepped plinth, with its barrels along the tile's side. Angled buttress fins flare its base where it stands on a surface. Do not wrap a lone Defense tile in sloped armour on every face: that reads as a shapeless blob.

Armor alone should not be the only identifier: heavily armoured variants of other families must still exist. Defense needs its emplacement or protected observation feature.

### Command and Communications

A mast, dish, antenna group, or sensor array provides the main identifier, usually on a small raised or stepped mounting. Give this family a deliberate focal point with orderly surrounding surfaces.

Observation glazing can appear, but the sensor silhouette remains dominant so it does not become another Habitat cube. Variants should retain a conspicuous sensor element even when their panel arrangement changes.

Tall features need restraint: a small module should have equipment proportionate to its 5 m body. Larger arrays span several cubes as settlement landmarks: a 2 × 2 block of Command tiles raises one large dish.

### Corridor

Make the passage visible from outside through a deep, framed opening or a projecting connector collar. The opening communicates a route through or between modules; repeated portal ribs communicate direction.

Straight, corner, T-junction, and crossroads variants can respond to connected neighbors. Their exterior shell, framing, and exposed ends should express those connections. No detailed interior layout is needed for this stage.

A dark recess can suggest passage depth, but it should read as an opening rather than a black rectangle painted on a wall. Retain a clear threshold and frame. Where the environment requires sealing, an exposed end can show a closed door or an exterior airlock interface.

Round portholes along both sides let people walking through see out. Where a corridor ends in open space, its end becomes a docking collar with a round hatch for small craft. Where it ends against a tile, that tile's face becomes an octagonal socket that the corridor's collar plugs into.

### Landing Pad

A round deck raised on a pedestal and angled struts, with a rim and an airlock hatch at its centre, so a small craft docks straight onto the way into the station. The pad sits on the tile's main face; its other faces carry a window row for the pad's control room.

Four pads in a 2 × 2 block form one large pad of about 9 m, each tile building its quarter. Blocks are paired on even cells of the station grid, so every tile in a block agrees on the same large pad. A single pad takes a pod about 6 m long; the large pad takes a shuttle about 11 m long. Larger ships still use Storage and Logistics hangars, which develop across several cubes.

The round deck breaks the block grid and stays readable from any angle. Pads can sit on any exposed face, including faces pointing away from the rest of the structure.

### Conduit

An open cell, like a Corridor, that carries a bundle of two large pipes and a smaller one along the surface beneath it on saddles. It is not solid, so the faces beside it stay exposed.

Conduits link to neighbouring conduits that share the same surface. A single link runs straight through; two links at a right angle make a curved elbow; three or more meet at a junction block. At each end, a run plugs into a manifold block that the receiving tile builds on its face, with heavy collars where the pipes meet the block. A run that ends in open space finishes in a blind flange. Where there is no surface below, the pipes cross the gap unsupported as a pipe bridge.

Conduits make the relationship between Power and the tiles it feeds physical. Their long tubes and round elbows also break up the blocky massing.

### Truss

An open cell, like a Corridor or Conduit, holding a heavy space frame: massive and brutalist rather than a modern station's light lattice. Chords are 0.8 m box beams with raised splice bands, joints are 1.3 m cast nodes, and diagonal braces are flat members with gusset plates at their ends.

Trusses side by side make one frame. Each member is built once and stays inside the truss cells, flush with their outer surface, so nothing reaches into a neighbour's armour. Faces onto open space are braced, the diagonal alternating from cell to cell so a run zigzags; faces between trusses stay open. A solid face beside a truss becomes a plain armour bearing plate the frame stands on, with no relief or family form, and a truss counts as covering it, so main faces and joined rows avoid it. Corridors do not link into trusses.

A run reads as a hollow girder, a stack as a lattice tower, a cantilever as a boom. The frame is built from the cells' real corners, so it follows wedge and apex cells too: a wedge becomes a triangular girder with a king post, an apex an inverted pyramid. Members keep their size in cells 4.2 m and wider and thin in proportion below that.

### Hull and Structure

Hull carries the station-wide relief and nothing else. This family establishes the supporting mass and gives the more distinctive tiles room to stand out. Where hull cubes stand free of side neighbours as columns, they become towers and get angled buttresses at their base.

Where a hull wall rises from a surface, a sloped fillet about 1.3 m deep runs along its foot. Neighbours with the same edge continue the fillet, so stepped tiers and trench walls read as battered armour rather than hard steps, in the manner of a Star Destroyer's superstructure.

Its identity is solid enclosure. It should have neither Habitat's window rows nor Logistics' large shutter. A pair of small windows on some faces, round maintenance hatches, and restrained ribs provide scale without making it another machinery or habitat tile.

## Openings and exposed faces

An opening is a visible feature on a tile face. A Corridor tile is a module whose purpose is connection. A Habitat entrance can connect to a Corridor, and two adjacent modules can also connect directly.

Use approximately 1 m × 2.1 m for a human doorway and 1.5–2 m × 2.4 m for a visible corridor passage as initial scale references. Larger cargo openings belong to Logistics or a facility spanning several cubes.

Apply exterior treatment to exposed faces. Faces against solid neighbours are not built, so shared faces cannot retain floating facade details, blocked windows, or equipment trapped between neighboring modules. Corridors and conduits are open connectors: the faces beside them stay exposed and keep their treatment.

A connection arriving at a face takes that face over, so the face reads as the place where the connection plugs in:

- A corridor ending against the face turns it into an octagonal socket for the corridor's collar.
- A conduit ending against it turns it into a manifold block for the pipes.

The face drops its family's usual form, and the relief still fills whatever the socket or block leaves free. At exposed corners, frames and equipment mounts should terminate cleanly or wrap intentionally.

A tile should retain recognizable identity from the camera angles used for construction. Do not put the entire identity on a single face that commonly becomes hidden. Habitat can use glazing on multiple exposed faces; machinery types can repeat a simplified equipment motif on a secondary face; Command can use the silhouette of the tower on its main face.

On curved assemblies, follow the local cube orientation for panels and mounting frames. Keep adjacent connection frames aligned. Human-scale details should remain recognizable instead of stretching arbitrarily with the shell.

## Neighboring tiles and larger structures

Individual tiles remain legible through their family forms, not their cube outlines, while compatible neighbors form a larger visual composition. Align selected bands, frames, conduits, and equipment strips across boundaries; avoid making every cube a completely isolated ornament.

| Combination | Exterior relationship |
| --- | --- |
| Habitat + Habitat | Window rows and visors join into continuous lit bands, creating a residential row; stacked homes add two bands per cube. |
| Habitat + Corridor | The corridor's collar plugs into an octagonal socket on the home's face. |
| Industrial + Industrial | Machinery strips, gantries, and service recesses continue into a larger workshop facade. |
| Industrial + Storage | A broad loading frontage meets machinery and service equipment. |
| Power + Industrial | Thick conduits visibly connect equipment banks to a machinery cluster. |
| Life Support + Habitat | Organized service connections terminate at the home's shell while its windows remain dominant. |
| Logistics + Logistics | Cargo frames and reinforcing bands combine into a larger loading facility. |
| Defense + Hull | A reinforced platform provides a quiet base for a clear emplacement silhouette. |
| Command + Defense | A sensor focal point relates visually to nearby outward-facing emplacements. |
| Conduit + Power or Industrial | Pipe runs leave equipment banks and plug into manifold blocks on the tiles they feed. |
| Landing Pad × 4 | A 2 × 2 block of pads becomes one large pad with a single central hatch. |
| Corridor + open space | The open end becomes a docking collar with a round hatch. |

A large factory, hangar, energy installation, communications array, or communal facility can occupy several cubes. These larger assemblies supply landmarks and break up repetition. They remain proportionate to the settlement rather than inheriting the Death Star's planet-scale machinery.

### 2 × 2 landmarks

Per-tile forms stick out only one or two metres, so from a distance a station reads as a box with bumps. Large multi-cell pieces give it a silhouette.

When four tiles of the same family, with the same orientation, form a 2 × 2 block whose main faces are all exposed, the block becomes one landmark:

| Family | Landmark |
| --- | --- |
| Command and Communications | A dish about 9 m across, tilted on a pedestal and yoke, with a feed on a tripod |
| Power and Thermal | A reactor dome on a ring drum, between two ribbed radiator wings about 6 m tall |
| Life Support | A sphere tank about 7 m across on a drum, with an equator band |
| Storage and Logistics | One hangar mouth about 8 m × 7 m and 4.4 m deep, framed by sloped lips |
| Landing Pad | One round pad about 9 m across with a single central hatch |

Rules:

- Blocks pair cells on even coordinates of the station grid, so every tile of a block agrees on which block it belongs to.
- The block's tiles lay a quiet plinth. The anchor tile, the one even on both axes, builds any piece shared across the block.
- Hangar mouths and pads are split, each tile building its own quarter.
- Tiles not in a complete block keep their single-tile forms, so a landmark appears only once the player completes the block.

## Foundry neighbourhood solver

The Foundry stores a family per occupied cell (save version 6; older saves become Hull). `SettlementSolver`
derives the forms from current occupancy, independently of placement order. No WFC is needed for this palette.

- A face-connected family group scores all 24 `(axis, side)` orientations. Solid backing and open main faces
  favour outward installations; in-plane neighbours favour pad decks and frontages; axial neighbours favour
  Defense towers. Storage aligns its slot with the row, Life Support aligns its vault, and connectors follow
  their links. Coordinate hashes break equal scores and seed detail. No score refers to world up or the camera.
- The existing face and corner rules then choose the mesh: covered faces disappear, Habitat rows leave piers
  where they stop, continuous rows wrap compatible corners, and occupied edge diagonals create fillets or tower
  buttresses. Stacked mass forms terraces and trenches; solid spans get sloped arch haunches where they meet
  their supports. Defense columns carry a turret only on the exposed axial tip.
- Complete even-grid 2 × 2 groups share one pad, hangar or other family landmark; removing a quarter restores
  individual forms. Storage rows merge into slots and Life Support rows become vaults. Corridor branches join
  at pressure junctions; nonplanar Conduit groups use pipe manifolds with links on all six faces. Corridor and
  Conduit remain open cells, so adjacent solid walls stay exposed.
- An edit re-solves the affected family relationships and compares the placements with the previous solution.
  Changed orientations, removed cells and a two-cell neighbourhood rebuild together, including diagonal
  dependants and cells across rendering chunks. Undo and load use the same path. A group can reorient when its
  shape changes; restoring identical coordinates and families restores identical architecture.
- Rendering uses 5 m per guide unit. Tile geometry samples the shared eight-corner lattice of `BlockGuide.Map`;
  shared assemblies sample by station position, including across cell boundaries. The relief is still generated
  once conceptually in station space: its plane coordinates and seeds never restart at a cube edge. Interpolated
  surface normals keep its 0.1–0.8 m depth in metres. Fixed-size parts are true size at their anchor (the shared
  tangent metric pads and hatches already use), and window rows adjust pane count and height to the available
  physical wall. Stretchy parts and large shells follow the curvature.

`tools/tile-lab.sh report` includes `foundry*.png`, generated from family-labelled occupancy through the same
solver and renderer as placement. `tools/tile-lab.sh validate` checks save migration, all cube orientations,
insertion order, connector openness, shared-corner/relief continuity, scale compensation, and actual Foundry
placement, pad completion, removal, undo/redo and picking in an isolated edit-mode scene.

## Ridges, keels and crowns

The Foundry's sphere map folds the cells on the cube's diagonals. Each cell's shape comes from its eight corners (`BlockConstruction.Shape`):

| Shape | Where | Form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Box | Everywhere else, about 85% of a skin | Sheared and 2.3–5 m across, but still a box: it carries the family forms above |
| Wedge | Along the 12 cube-edge arcs | A triangular prism with a near-equilateral ~5.5 m cross-section, 2.5–3.9 m long. Two faces fold flat into one nearly flat roof band; the other two slopes meet underneath in a sharp 46–60° keel |
| Apex | On the 8 cube-corner diagonals | A tetrahedron cap: a curved triangular crown with 4.3–4.8 m edges over a point about 5 m deep |

How a wedge or apex meets open space decides what it builds (`BlockConstruction.Exposure`):

| Exposure | When | Built as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ridge | The whole roof is open: the normal outer skin | Crest, plus any open slopes and gables |
| Half | Part of the roof is covered: the open part is the foot of the neighbour's wall | A fillet with family trim |
| Keel | The roof is covered and the slopes are open | Slopes and a keel strip |
| Gable | An end face along the ridge is open: the end of a run | Gable |
| Crown | An apex's roof is open | Crown |
| Spike | An apex's roof is covered and its slopes are open | Three slopes |

Each family has a kit of four parts, built for these shapes rather than squeezed out of a box:

- **Crest:** on a wedge's roof. It joins along the ridge with wedges of the same family and caps the ends of a run. The roof itself is nearly flat; the crest is what makes the band read as a ridge.
- **Slope:** on one open slope, on ridges and on both sides of a keel.
- **Gable:** on an open triangular end, about 5.5 m on each side.
- **Crown:** on an apex's roof. It links to up to three ridges of the same family, or to connectors.

Keel strips (an armoured edge with running lights and a hatch every second cell), half fillets and spikes are shared parts with family trim.

The 12 ridges and 8 crowns are the station's large-scale structure, like the spine lines and trench of the reference films. Crowns are landmarks that read on the limb from far away. Ridges vary along their length by coordinate hash (lit and dark bays, fin heights in a wave, turrets on every other cell), so a long ridge never reads as copies.

| Family | Crest | Slope | Gable | Crown |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hull | Armoured keel spine, vertebra ribs on a 1.25 m pitch, running lights | Armour courses falling from the crest in chevrons; maintenance hatch | Triangular armour plate with a round EVA hatch | Three spines meet at a raised hub with a beacon |
| Habitat | A-frame gallery: a glazed ridge cap lit from inside | Eye-level window rows under a visor | Tall triangular window wall with mullions and a railed balcony | Three-sided glazed lantern with a deck inside and a walkway round it |
| Industrial | Twin crane rails and a travelling gantry crane | Conveyor along the eave with chutes | Loading bay door with a jib crane | Tripod derrick: lattice legs to the three crown corners, boom on top |

Rules:

- Kit parts follow the fixed-size rules of [Scale and human reference](#scale-and-human-reference). They are built directly from the cell's real corners in a rigid frame, so their edges meet the bent neighbours exactly.
- 2 × 2 landmarks form only on four box cells.
- Every family fits every cell. A corridor keeps its true-size tube in the narrowest (2.4 m) cells, overhanging into its neighbours by at most 0.2 m (collars 0.4 m). A landing pad's deck takes the largest size class that overhangs its face by at most 0.4 m a side (L from 3.6 m, M from 2.6 m, S below); a 2 × 2 pad takes its class from the block's width, and the hatch stays true size. Other families' smallest size fits the smallest cells, and every family has a kit.

Every solid family builds its own kit on special cells (`TileBuilder.Kits*.cs`). Corridors and conduits keep their tubes, junctions and collars there: a corridor on a ridge in a skin always links to its neighbours, so it is a junction, not a gallery. The Foundry's shape view (C, twice) and its **Ridges** example show where the special cells are.

## Angled and round forms

A grid of cubes easily looks blocky. Break it with angles and curves at every scale, while keeping the cube grid underneath:

| Scale | Angled | Round |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relief | Ramps, buttresses on arcs and wells | Drums, domes, rings, ribbed arcs across several tiles, and wells, set in a flattened bed |
| Family forms | Sloped armour courses (Defense), sloped hangar lips (Storage), angled window visors, a sloped bridge window (Command), angled pad struts; A-frame galleries and triangular gable walls on ridges | Vaults and tanks (Life Support), round pads, conduit elbows, round hatches and portholes, sensor globes |
| Landmarks | Radiator wings; on crowns, a tripod derrick, three radiator wings in a Y and a three-turret bastion | A large dish, a reactor dome, a sphere tank; a geodesic dome on a crown |
| Junctions | Angled buttress fins where towers and turret mounts stand on a surface; sloped fillets along the foot of hull walls | Docking collars, octagonal corridor sockets |

Buttresses go on Defense and Command tiles, and on hull columns that stand free of side neighbours. Each gets two angled fins on each concave edge where its face meets the surface it stands on, like the flared bases of the turret towers on the Death Star trench models. Fins follow the face, not a world direction, so they work in any orientation.

## Detail hierarchy and materials

Design at three scales:

1. **Settlement scale:** overall mass, clusters, gaps, terraces, connector routes, and larger landmarks.
2. **Cube scale:** the defining window rows, shutter, machine bank, tank group, emplacement, pad, pipe bundle, or portal.
3. **Close scale:** frame thickness, brackets, pipe joints, grilles, seams, markings, and wear.

The middle scale carries tile recognition. Fine detail should reinforce it and should not overwhelm the main feature.

Start with a restrained family of painted materials, such as grays and off-whites, then add tile-specific contrast through glass, dark recesses, ribs, and metal equipment. The carved armour plates are the close-scale detail of flat armour; round forms stay smooth. The settlement can have its own visual identity; Imperial styling is an influence rather than a requirement.

Use stronger category colors in an optional planning overlay. Small exterior accents and lights can support wayfinding, but large color blocks should not substitute for distinct geometry.

Use controlled variation in panel placement, equipment arrangement, and small fittings. Keep major windows, openings, and connection points deliberate. Repeated tiles should look related without appearing mechanically identical.

## Variants and conditions

Begin with a small number of exterior variants per family. Every variant must preserve its identifying feature. Rotation and neighboring connections add variation, but they should not erase function.

Treat unfinished, damaged, and heavily armoured as conditions that can apply across families:

- **Unfinished:** missing shell plates, visible support framing, and incomplete exterior equipment.
- **Damaged:** localized broken glazing, dented panels, interrupted equipment, or damaged frames while enough of the original signature remains readable.
- **Heavily armoured:** additional plates and protective frames that preserve the family's main identifying feature.

Medical, garden, detention, computing, and other specialized facilities can be introduced later if they earn both a distinct function and a recognizable exterior. Avoid adding picker entries whose only difference is a name or decal.

## Visual checks for the first prototype

Build one clear exemplar of each core family before producing many variants. Review the tiles together at the normal construction camera distance.

- Can Habitat be identified immediately by its rows of windows?
- Do window rows sit in their 2.5 m bands and line up across neighbouring tiles?
- Do landing pads read as places for craft to dock, and conduits as pipe runs between tiles?
- Does each family stay identifiable in any of its orientations, with no face reading as a roof or a front because of where it points?
- Does a wide area of relief show no tile seams, with both quiet and dense districts?
- Do angles and curves break up the blocky grid without hiding the family forms?
- Do arcs and wells sit in the relief, in their bed, rather than on top of it?
- Does flat armour read as carved plates up close, with the split plates standing out as two pieces?
- Does the station read as one armoured mass in space rather than as buildings on ground?
- Do 2 × 2 landmarks give the station a silhouette from a distance, and does a lone tile of each family still read without forming a lump?
- Do corridors and conduits visibly plug into the faces they reach?
- Do docked craft show pads and hangar mouths at the right size?
- Can the other families be identified without labels or category colors?
- Do Power, Life Support, and Industrial remain distinct through regular banks, grouped vessels, and asymmetrical machinery respectively?
- Do Corridor openings read as passages and Logistics shutters as cargo access?
- Does each family remain identifiable from common oblique and elevated camera angles?
- Do a person and doorway establish the same scale across all tiles?
- Do repeated and mixed clusters look connected without losing individual tile identity?
- Does plain gray lighting reveal meaningful depth, including frames, recesses, ledges, and equipment mounts?
- Do the large features survive when zooming out, without fine detail becoming distracting visual noise?

Use grazing light early to check physical relief. Reserve geometry for silhouette, meaningful shadows, and visible depth; use surface maps for smaller markings and fine texture. Judge the result at the actual camera distance, including during movement.

## Research basis and design scope

[Death Star operation and technical research](death-star-research.md) provides the original separation of accommodation, industry, power, life support, logistics, defense, and command functions.

[Death Star visual design and film production research](death-star-visual-design-and-film-production-research.md) provides the visual principles: simple strong forms, mechanical assemblies that suggest function, physical relief, controlled repetition, quiet areas between dense detail, restrained paint, and lighting that reveals structure.

Reference photographs of modular, 3D-printed Death Star trench models informed the arcs, round ports, sloped buttresses, and long tubes.

The 5 m cubes, one-person homes, the no-up rule, the station-wide relief, window rows, landing pads, conduits, and distinct exterior signatures are the settlement design proposed here. Interiors follow the rules under Interiors and windows: one room per cube with its floor towards the core, sliding doors, lifts and a zero-g hub. Furnishings, resource simulation, and staffing remain outside this exterior design scope.
